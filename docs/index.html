Analyzing Pedagogical Feedback with a BERT‑based Classifier

Audit Method Results Code

BEA Workshop at ACL 2025 (Association for Computational Linguistics)

An audit of our team’s submission to the shared task at the 2025 BEA workshop, which placed 28th of 44, and an upgraded version of it. I fixed how it was evaluated, had it read the tutor’s reply before the dialogue so the reply is never cut off, weighted the rare classes in training and tuned its decision threshold, raising macro‑F1 from 0.576 to 0.669.

What went wrong? How was it measured? How much better is it? What is uncertain?

Summary

In April 2025 our team, ALA, entered the BEA 2025 shared task with a fine-tuned BERT model that judges whether an AI tutor's reply recognises a student's mistake. It scored macro-F1 0.636 on the hidden test set, 28th of 44. Rebuilding it showed that the number was partly luck. Its validation set leaked, long dialogues lost the reply being judged, nothing countered the class imbalance, and under an honest evaluation the same recipe scores 0.576, below a bag-of-words baseline that trains in seconds.

I fixed the measurement first, then the model. The new system reads the reply before the history, trains with a class-weighted loss and a tuned schedule, and moves its decision threshold toward the rare classes. Under the same evaluation it scores 0.669, and its F1 on the hardest class, “To some extent”, rises from 0.10 to 0.34. Two additions that sounded promising, multi-task supervision and a four-model ensemble, made no measurable difference.

The task

Language models are increasingly used as maths tutors, and a good tutor's first job when a student gets something wrong is to notice. The BEA 2025 shared task [1], held with ACL 2025, asked teams to judge tutor replies automatically. Each example is the end of a tutoring dialogue in which the student has just made a mistake, followed by one tutor's next reply. Track 1, Mistake Identification, asks whether that reply recognises the mistake:

  • Yes: the mistake is clearly identified.
  • To some extent: the reply suggests there may be a mistake but sounds unsure.
  • No: the reply does not recognise it, for example by praising the answer or moving on.

Every dialogue comes with replies from up to nine tutors: seven language models and one or two humans. One development-set dialogue shows the range:

DialogueTutor: What is 163 − 156?  Student: 13

GPT-4 · Yes“You're close, but let's try again. Remember, when you subtract 156 from 163, you should get 7.”

Human novice · To some extent“Kindly recheck your answer.”

Gemini · To some extent“That's a great start! Let's double-check that subtraction, 163 − 156 equals 7.”

Phi-3 · No“That's correct! Now, can you try solving 204 − 189?”

The Gemini reply states the right answer yet was labelled “To some extent”, presumably because it opens with praise. The labels are human judgements with substantial but imperfect agreement (Fleiss' κ of about 0.65 across the four dimensions [1]), so no system can be expected to match them exactly.

Systems are ranked by macro-averaged F1 over the three classes, which weights the rare classes as much as the common one:

\[ \mathrm{F1}_{\text{macro}} = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{c\,\in\,\{\text{Yes},\,\text{Some},\,\text{No}\}} \frac{2\,P_c R_c}{P_c + R_c}, \]

where \(P_c\) and \(R_c\) are the precision and recall of class \(c\). A “lenient” variant merges Yes and To some extent. Always answering Yes scores 0.283 on the test set.

Data

The organisers released a labelled development set of 300 dialogues with 2,476 tutor replies, drawn from the MathDial [3] and Bridge [4] corpora (MRBench [2]), and an unlabelled test set of 191 dialogues with 1,547 replies. There is no separate training set: everything a system learns comes from the 2,476 development replies. The test labels were never released; scores come from the organisers' submission server.

Three properties of the data shaped everything that follows.

  • Replies come in groups. All replies to a dialogue share its history, the student's mistake and often its numbers. Thirteen pairs of dialogues even share an identical history under different ids, so the 300 dialogues contain only 287 distinct conversations.
  • The rare classes decide the score. “To some extent” has 174 examples. Because macro-F1 weights it equally, a system's rank is set mostly by how it handles the 544 replies that are not Yes.
  • The test set differs. Its dialogues are longer (median 391 tokens in the submitted system's format, against 335), and its labels are less one-sided. The organisers' majority-class baseline (accuracy 0.7363 strict, 0.8255 lenient, on 1,547 replies) pins down the hidden test labels' mix exactly: 1,139 Yes, 138 To some extent and 270 No, that is 73.6%, 8.9% and 17.5%, against 78.0%, 7.0% and 14.9% in development.

The submitted system

Our team, ALA, entered Track 1 in April 2025 with a fine-tuned BERT classifier [5] written in a Colab notebook:

  • Input: one string, "Conversation history: {history}\nTutor: {response}", cut at 512 tokens.
  • Model: bert-base-uncased with a three-way classification head.
  • Training: the Hugging Face Trainer defaults with a learning rate of 1×10−5, batches of 16 and 4 epochs, on a random 80% of the development replies. The weights after the last epoch were used.
  • Validation: the other 20%, 496 replies: macro-F1 0.643, accuracy 86.3%.

On the hidden test set it scored macro-F1 0.636 (accuracy 84.2%; lenient macro-F1 0.849), 28th of the 44 teams on the official leaderboard. The winner scored 0.718.

Reproducing it

I re-implemented the notebook's training exactly (same tokenizer, input string, optimiser, schedule, batch size, epochs and the same 496 validation replies, replayed index for index from its random split) and retrained it on a laptop GPU. Training loss averaged 0.508 over the run against the notebook's 0.527, but the validation score was not reproduced: macro-F1 0.543, not 0.643. The reason is the rarest class. The retrained model predicted “To some extent” for 1 of 496 validation replies and 2 of 1,547 test replies, so its F1 on that class, a third of the score, was zero. Two more retrainings with different random seeds scored 0.517 and 0.638. Only the second predicted “To some extent” at all (9 times), and only it came close to the notebook's 0.643. The recipe barely learns the class, whether it predicts it at all depends on the random seed, and the submitted model happened to land on the better side.

What went wrong

Reading the notebook again, and re-running it, turned up five problems. Each one either hid how good the model really was or limited how good it could be.

1. The validation set leaked

The 80/20 split was drawn over individual replies, so the eight or nine replies to a dialogue were scattered across both sides. Every one of the 496 validation replies had siblings from the same dialogue in the training data. The test set, by contrast, consists of dialogues the model has never seen. A validation score measured this way answers an easier question than the one the leaderboard asks, and it cannot be trusted to choose between models.

Measured directly, the leak did little to this particular recipe. Under five-fold cross-validation split by reply, as the notebook split its data, it scores 0.567 (0.542–0.595); with each conversation kept in one fold, 0.576 (0.545–0.608). A model that barely learns the rare classes has little to gain from having seen a sibling reply, so the notebook's 0.643 owes more to the seed lottery above than to the leak. The leak matters all the same: models that do use the reply's wording gain about three points from it (the table in section 5), enough to change which of two models looks better.

2. Long dialogues lost the reply being judged

The input put the dialogue history first and the reply last, then cut everything after token 512. When a dialogue is long, the cut falls on the reply, the only part of the input the label is about. This mattered far more on the test set than in development: 186 test inputs (12%) were cut against 94 (4%), and for 40 test replies nothing of the reply survived, so the model had to guess from the history alone.

3. Nothing addressed the imbalance

Plain cross-entropy on a 78% majority class rewards predicting Yes. Neither the loss nor the decision rule did anything for the two classes that decide macro-F1.

4. Settings that did nothing

  • eval_steps=500 was set, but evaluation was never switched on, so the model was not evaluated during training.
  • Training ran 496 steps, fewer than the 500 between checkpoints, so no checkpoint was ever saved and model selection was impossible.
  • The final model was never retrained on all the labelled data, leaving 20% of an already small set unused.
  • The tokenizer was reloaded from distilbert-base-uncased for the BERT model. I checked this one: the two tokenizers produce identical ids, so it did no harm.

5. A much simpler model did better

The team never compared BERT with a simple baseline. A bag-of-words model, TF-IDF features of the reply alone fed to a logistic regression, takes seconds to train. Under the honest evaluation below it scores macro-F1 0.624, against 0.576 for the submitted recipe. Most of what the fine-tuned transformer knew could be read off the reply's words.

Evaluation protocol

Before changing the model I fixed the measurement, because a better model is only recognisable with a trustworthy score.

  • Conversation-grouped cross-validation. The 2,476 development replies are split into five folds such that every reply to a conversation (identical histories count as one conversation) sits in the same fold, with the label mix balanced across folds. Each fold is predicted by a model trained on the other four, giving one honest prediction for every reply. This is not a new idea: the fifth-placed team, BD, also grouped its folds by dialogue [10].
  • One number per system. Macro-F1 is computed over all 2,476 pooled predictions. The 95% intervals come from a bootstrap that resamples whole conversations, 2,000 times, since replies to the same conversation are not independent. Differences between two systems are bootstrapped on the same resamples, which makes them much tighter than the individual intervals.
  • Calibrated against reality. The old system is run through the same protocol, so its cross-validated score can be compared with its known official test score of 0.636.
  • The test set is used once. All design decisions were made on cross-validation. The final system's test predictions are to be scored once on the organisers' post-competition submission server; that score is not in yet.

Run through the protocol, the submitted recipe scores 0.576 (0.545–0.608), well below both its printed validation score and its test score. That is consistent with the reproduction above: the recipe's typical model predicts “To some extent” almost never (17 times in 2,476 replies here), and the submitted model was a fortunate exception. Comparing new systems against this recipe through the same protocol is the fair comparison; comparing them with 0.636 would credit the old system with its luck.

New method

The new system keeps the old one's basic shape, a fine-tuned transformer encoder, and changes what the audit pointed at. Each change is a separate step so its effect can be measured on its own.

Input: the reply first, never cut

The reply becomes the first segment of a sentence pair and the history the second. If the pair is longer than 512 tokens, the history is shortened from the middle: it keeps its first 96 tokens, which hold the problem statement, and as much of its end as fits, which holds the student's latest turn and so the mistake itself. The two parts are joined by an ellipsis. A reply longer than 256 tokens is cut to its first 252, which only matters for degenerate ones: the 15 or 16 development replies this affects (depending on the tokenizer) all come from Phi-3, which kept generating invented follow-up turns after its actual reply, and the reply itself is at the start. On the test set, between 199 and 331 histories are shortened this way, depending on the model's tokenizer.

Training recipe

A learning rate of 2×10−5 with a 10% linear warm-up, and a class-weighted loss. The weights are proportional to \(1/\sqrt{n_c}\), where \(n_c\) is the number of training examples of class \(c\), normalised to average 1 over the training set: 0.74 for Yes, 2.46 for To some extent and 1.69 for No. This is a deliberately milder correction than full inverse-frequency weighting (which would weight the rarest class eleven times the majority), because the decision rule below makes a final, data-driven adjustment.

Multi-task supervision

Every development reply is also labelled for three other dimensions of the same taxonomy [2]: whether it locates the mistake, provides guidance, and is actionable. These are closely tied to mistake identification (366 of the 370 “No” replies also fail to locate the mistake) and come free with the data. The classifier gets three extra three-way heads, trained alongside the main one; only the Mistake Identification head is used to predict. For a batch \(B\) the loss is

\[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{\sum_{i\in B} w_{y_i}\,\ell_{\text{MI}}(i)}{\sum_{i\in B} w_{y_i}} \;+\; \frac{\lambda}{3}\sum_{d\,\in\,\{\text{ML},\,\text{PG},\,\text{AC}\}} \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i\in B}\ell_{d}(i), \qquad \lambda = 0.5, \]

where each \(\ell\) is a cross-entropy and \(w\) the class weights above.

Backbones and ensemble

The full recipe is trained on four encoders of similar size: BERT, RoBERTa [6], MPNet [7] (the all-mpnet-base-v2 checkpoint [8] used by the fifth-placed team [10]) and ModernBERT [9]. Each is trained five times, once per cross-validation fold, and the final system averages the class probabilities of all of them. The ensemble was fixed in advance, before any result was seen, as every full-recipe backbone that finished. Under cross-validation each reply is predicted only by models that never saw its conversation.

Decision rule

A model trained with cross-entropy picks the most probable class, which is not the choice that maximises macro-F1. The last step adds a constant to the log-probabilities of the two minority classes before taking the largest:

\[ \hat y = \arg\max_{c}\; \big(\log \bar p_c + b_c\big), \qquad b_{\text{Yes}} = 0, \]

where \(\bar p\) is the ensemble's averaged probability and \(b_{\text{Some}}\), \(b_{\text{No}}\) are chosen by grid search to maximise macro-F1. The reported score is cross-fitted: the offsets used for each fold are tuned on the other four folds only. The offsets used on the test set are tuned on all 2,476 out-of-fold predictions.

Results

Under the evaluation protocol the final system scores macro-F1 0.669 (0.641–0.698), against 0.576 for the submitted recipe, a difference of +0.093 (+0.064 to +0.123). The decision rule helps the old recipe too: given the same rule it reaches 0.606, and the like-for-like gain is +0.063 (+0.039 to +0.091). The reply-only TF-IDF baseline from the audit, 0.624, is the more sobering reference: the new system clears it by about four and a half points.

What each step contributed

Differences below are paired: both systems are scored on the same bootstrap resamples of conversations, with the decision rule applied to both.

  • Input. Reply first, history trimmed from the middle: 0.619 against 0.606 (+0.013, interval −0.018 to +0.045). Within noise here, which is expected: only 94 development inputs were long enough for the old format to cut, against 12% of the test set. Without the rule this model never predicted “To some extent” at all, which is why its hollow marker sits so low.
  • Training recipe. Warm-up, a higher learning rate and the class weights: 0.650 (+0.031, interval +0.001 to +0.061), the largest single step and the only one on BERT whose interval excludes zero.
  • Multi-task heads. 0.640 (−0.010, interval −0.028 to +0.008): no gain. The extra labels made the probabilities rank “To some extent” slightly better (one-vs-rest AUC 0.706 to 0.713) without improving the decisions. The full recipe was fixed before any result was seen, so the heads stay in the final system; this result says they could be dropped.
  • Backbones. With the full recipe and the rule, RoBERTa scores 0.660, MPNet 0.663 and ModernBERT 0.632. MPNet, the backbone the fifth-placed team chose [10], is the strongest single model.
  • Ensemble. Averaging all four: 0.669, which is +0.007 (−0.015 to +0.029) above MPNet alone with the same rule. The ensemble is no worse than its best member and cost four times the training; on this evidence a single MPNet would do.

Where the gain comes from

Almost all of it is the rarest class. F1 for “To some extent” rises from 0.10 to 0.34 and for “No” from 0.70 to 0.75, while “Yes” barely moves (0.92 to 0.91). The tuned rule adds 1.0 to the log-probability of “To some extent” and 0.3 to that of “No”: the system needs much less evidence before it calls a reply uncertain.

The same trade shows by slice. On the longest dialogues, the ones the old format cut, accuracy rises from 79% to 84%; elsewhere it drops by one to three points, the price of predicting the rare classes more often. Replies from human tutors are the hardest for both systems. For expert tutors accuracy falls (73% to 63%) while macro-F1 rises (0.31 to 0.43), because the old recipe never found their “To some extent” replies; for novices both rise.

What did not help

Replies to the same dialogue are judged against the same mistake, so a reply that is vaguer than its siblings may be the uncertain one. An exploratory second stage gave a logistic regression each reply's ensemble probabilities together with summaries of its siblings'. It scored 0.659, which is −0.010 against the ensemble alone, and the sibling information itself was worth +0.002 (−0.010 to +0.014) against a control with only the reply's own probabilities. It is not part of the final system.

On the test set

The test labels were never released, so the test score has to come from the organisers' post-competition server, and it is not in yet. The final system's predictions for the 1,547 test replies are ready: they call 153 replies “To some extent” and 261 “No”, against 138 and 270 in the hidden labels, where a replica of the submitted model calls 2 and 242. The two systems agree on 85% of test replies and on 80% of the 186 whose reply the old format cut.

Limitations

  • Little data. 2,476 replies in 287 conversations. The 95% intervals are about three points either side, so steps of a point or two cannot be told from noise.
  • Cross-validation is not the test set. The test dialogues are longer and their labels less one-sided, and the decision rule's offsets were tuned to the development label mix; they may over- or under-call the rare classes on test.
  • Choices made after looking. The steps of the method were chosen after the audit, and the audit after seeing how the old system scored. Only the make-up of the final system was fixed before any result, and only the test score is free of tuning.
  • One seed per configuration under cross-validation. On a single split, three seeds of the old recipe ranged from 0.517 to 0.638; the new recipe's seed spread was not measured.
  • The labels. Annotators agree substantially but not perfectly (κ about 0.65 [1]), which caps what any system can score against them.

Reproducibility

The repository holds the code, one configuration file per model, every fold's held-out and test-set probabilities, and results/summary.json. Every number on this page is filled from that file when the page is built, and the build fails if one is missing, so the text cannot drift from the results. All training ran on a 16 GB Apple M2 laptop (PyTorch, MPS backend) under a thermal guard; a fold took 25 to 40 minutes and the whole set of runs about a day. The MRBench data are the organisers', under CC BY-SA 4.0, and are downloaded from their repository rather than redistributed.
